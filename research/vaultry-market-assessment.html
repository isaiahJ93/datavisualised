Hermes research · five independent fronts

Vaultry Market Assessment

Is there a legitimate gap in helping civil-construction subcontractors win work and manage their documents and compliance — and can it produce a large outcome?

  • Australia & New Zealand
  • Civil, not residential
  • Prototype stage
  • September 2026

Verdict

No to the venture as framed. Yes to a smaller, real business — and there is one genuinely interesting adjacent opportunity the research surfaced by accident.

Five agents worked separate fronts — incumbents, demand channels, market size, business model, regulation — without seeing each other's work. All five reached the same structural conclusion independently.

The party with the pain has no budget. The party with the budget wants the pain to continue. This is not a gap waiting to be filled. It is an equilibrium, and it is load-bearing for the incumbents.

§1

The two halves, judged separately

Help subcontractors get work No-go

This is measured, not argued. Felix (ASX:FLX) ran the experiment for 14 years with distribution no startup can buy: CPB Contractors and Downer mandate that new subcontractors register through it.

The corroboration is consistent. Oneflare: Domain paid $15m for 35%, Airtasker bought the assets for $9.8m, retired entirely 30 June 2026. hipages — the best-run trades marketplace in Australia, in a segment with 10–100× civil's job frequency — grew tradies 1% year on year at $2,497 ARPU, the proven ANZ ceiling for demand generation. Levelset sold to Procore for US$500m with 250,000 users and 1.3% paying, because what it actually sold was statutory lien deadlines, not leads.

Why it fails structurally: discovery is already free — GETS, EstimateOne's free tier, Austroads registers, tier-1 portals. The subcontractor's binding constraint is a gate, not a list: prequalification class, referee reports, a vendor record. Selling a better list to someone stopped by a gate is selling the wrong product.

Manage documents and compliance Crowded from both ends

The pitch “one profile, many head contractors” is already incumbent marketing copy:

  • Cm3 sells “one fee, one year, unlimited clients” — A$399–3,045/yr by risk tier
  • HammerTech (US$70m from Riverwood) is free to subcontractors
  • ComplyFlow is free to contractors and suppliers
  • Austroads National Prequalification System (Road & Bridge) — “no fee to apply”
  • Tōtika (CHASNZ) already solves duplication in New Zealand, industry-endorsed and JAS-ANZ audited

You cannot beat free that is funded by someone else's budget.

Who pays, settled: head contractors ≫ subcontractors under mandate > subcontractors voluntarily. LinkSafe is the proof — it sells principals a menu of who to bill. The payer is a setting in someone else's contract. No evidence was found anywhere of subcontractors voluntarily paying meaningfully for compliance absent a mandate. They pay for leads, for faster payment, or for capital.

The ceiling, arithmetically

Built bottom-up from counted units and cross-checked two ways that landed within 7% of each other — size-band arithmetic against CCF's 1,800 and CCNZ's 800 members at roughly 50% penetration.

TAM = (11,750 × A$900) + (4,230 × A$3,000) + (610 × A$15,000) + (28 × A$60,000)
    = A$34.1m/yr at 100% share

SAM = A$21.8m/yr        the ~4,868 firms with 5+ employees
SOM = A$2.2–4.4m ARR    over 5 years, 487–974 customers

And the market is contracting: engineering construction work done −5.1% year on year in the June 2026 quarter; Road & Bridge forecast −18% over two years; the transport pipeline falling from $11.4bn per quarter in early 2024 toward $5.2bn by 2029; NSW infrastructure down $2.2bn in real terms.

On “can this make me rich”

A venture-scale exit needs A$12–17m ARR. This market cannot produce it at any credible price.

What it can produce is roughly A$2–4m ARR at 80% margins with 3–6 people — about A$1.5–3m a year to an owner, trading at maybe A$8–20m. That is an excellent outcome if you own all of it, and a bad one if you raise institutional money against it, because the fund's case cannot be produced by this TAM. That distinction matters more than the number.

§3

Three findings that change how you would build

The pain is already a salary line, not a software line

Over half of small builders spend 5+ hours a week on regulatory tasks and 68% have considered scaling back over red tape — but 56% have already hired or redeployed staff to absorb it. There is no unspent compliance budget. You would be displacing a A$45–70k admin: a 3–6 month consultative sale, not a self-serve signup. A 20-person subcontractor already spends A$20–30k a year on software, so asking A$3–12k makes you a replacement — and you must name what you replace.

AI deflates this product rather than enabling it

Framework AI ships “Form Fill” — AI prequalification and tender form completion — in September 2026. If filling the form is free, the fee to store the form does not survive. HammerTech is spending its US$70m in the same direction.

The category dies by cheap absorption, not announcement

Which makes the failure rate look lower than it is: Damstra A$2.44 → A$0.24 and delisted; Felix −63% since IPO; Assignar moved its head office from Sydney to Denver; Sitemate took roughly nine years to a Series A. Every real exit in the space — Payapps ~US$390m, Levelset US$500m, BuildingConnected US$275m, Flashtract to Trimble — attaches to payments or bid flow, enterprise-paid. None is a document library.

§4

What actually survived

Three things, in ascending order of interest.

a. Invert the payer Proven, crowded

Sell supply-chain assurance to the principal; make the subcontractor's seat free. The only configuration with a demonstrated revenue base — roughly $99k per customer against $20 per vendor. It is also the configuration everyone is already in.

b. Currency, not forms Underserved

The law is forking, not converging. Victoria sits outside WHS harmonisation with its own security-of-payment model commenced 15 April 2026, retrospective to existing contracts; WA retention trusts from A$20,000 since 1 February 2024; Queensland frozen; New Zealand moving to a critical-risk model on 1 April 2027. Critically, NSW re-made its entire WHS Regulation (SL 2025 No 440), renumbering clauses to sections — so every SWMS and WHS plan in NSW citing “clause 291” is now stale. That is a re-papering event with a date. Forms are a commodity; knowing which version is current across eight jurisdictions is not.

c. The payment-documents wedge Strongest signal

Three agents reached this independently, from different directions:

  • Market sizing named “take a cut of flow — payments, retentions, insurance” as the only route where A$10m ARR comes from ~500 customers rather than ~4,000.
  • Business model found every real exit in the category attaches to payments or bid flow.
  • Regulation found Queensland explicitly named “lack of adequate accounting software to support trustees to comply” as a reason for pausing project trust account rollout — a government putting the software gap in writing.

Payment documentation is the one place where the document is not discretionary paperwork but the thing that releases cash: NSW security-of-payment supporting statements under s13(7)–(9), where omission is an offence and a knowingly false statement carries imprisonment; New Zealand statutory retention reporting to the subcontractor at least quarterly since 5 October 2023, with director penalties to $50k; WA retention trusts.

The caution: Queensland's trust rollout is paused with no new date, Payapps is already inside Autodesk, and this is adjacent to — not the same as — what Vaultry is prototyping today. It is a lead to validate, not a conclusion.

Kill shots, ranked

§6

The 30-day test — build nothing

Every memo independently recommended primary interviews over further desk research, because the decisive number does not exist publicly. No survey, association submission or inquiry was found quantifying how many portals a typical civil subcontractor pays for. The 3–5 estimate underpinning the entire “compliance tax” thesis is inference, not measurement.

  1. Pull the two datasets the research could not reach

    ABS CABEE cube (Division E × subdivision × employment size) and the ATO Small Business Benchmarks. Half a day, A$0.

    Stop if the real 5+ employee count is below 4,000.

  2. Twenty calls into the 20–199 employee band

    CCF and CCNZ rosters are a public, named universe. One question does most of the work: “Name an invoice you personally paid to get on site — the amount, and who sent it.”

    Stop if fewer than 5 of 10 can name one — the budget is upstream.

  3. Price test before building

    A one-page offer at A$1,250/mo to 30 firms, asking for a A$500 deposit.

    Stop if fewer than 3 of 30 deposit, or if more than half say “HammerTech already does that free.”

  4. Mandate test with five tier-1 procurement leads

    Would you accept a third-party evidence feed at all?

    Stop if 4+ are locked to Felix or Ariba. Downer has renewed to roughly FY29.

Green light: 5+ deposits and three or more firms independently describing the alternative as “hiring another admin.”

Do not repeat these three

1. A search summary claimed the SWMS fall-height threshold rose from 2m to 3m in July 2026. It could not be corroborated from any source and should not be repeated until checked.

2. The commencement date of the NSW WHS Regulation 2025 — sources conflict between 22 August 2025, a 1 September 2025 repeal deadline, and January 2026 commentary.

One credibility warning: the NSW Building Commission expansion (Fair Trading and Building Legislation Amendment Bill 2026, passed 5 August 2026) touches only residential and vertical construction. It is close to irrelevant to civil. Citing it as a tailwind in an investor conversation would be a credibility risk.
