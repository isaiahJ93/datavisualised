## VERDICT

**No-go on the Gulf. No-go on export generally, for at least 24 months. Not "not yet, keep
warming it up" — actively stop spending money and attention on it.**

The single biggest reason is not culture, not distance, and not competition. It is that
**civil construction is not a tradeable service.** You cannot ship earthmoving. "Exporting" civil
works means establishing a licensed, capitalised, classified, bonded operating entity inside the
target country — that is foreign direct investment, not export. The Saudi entry gate alone is a
contracting-licence paid-capital requirement of **SAR 500,000–2,000,000 ≈ A$205,000–A$821,000**
[S — [enterprisehub.sa](https://enterprisehub.sa/blog/company-formation-costs-in-saudi-arabia-for-foreigners-the-2026-strategic-cost-guide/)], which is **4x to 16x the client's entire capital**,
before a single tender, a single flight, or a single bond.

And the bond is the physical impossibility. Gulf oil and gas contracts routinely require a
**performance bond of 10% of contract value** [S — [OGDCL master tender set](https://ogdcl.com/sites/default/files/tender%20download/MasterSetofTenderDocument-Proprietary-Foreign-Updated-13-08-2021-BB-PBG-UPDATED.pdf)].
No bank or surety will issue an unsecured A$100,000 guarantee to a barely-trading company with
under A$50,000 of capital and no audited history. They will demand full cash cover. The client
cannot post it. The gate is closed at the arithmetic level, before anyone's opinion is involved.

If you want the number that should end the conversation: **CIMIC Group — Australia's largest
contractor — took a A$1.23 billion write-off on its 45% stake in BIC Contracting and exited the
Middle East** [S — [Arabian Business](https://www.arabianbusiness.com/industries/construction/453090-uae-construction-industry-in-crisis-more-casualties-loom-warns-expert)].
If a multi-billion-dollar ASX contractor with decades of track record could not make Gulf
construction work and left A$1.23bn behind, there is no version of this that a A$50,000 startup
wins. That is not pessimism. That is the base rate.

**What survives the analysis:** one reframe worth more than everything else in this memo. The
client's "international" ambition can be satisfied *without leaving Australia*, by selling to
**foreign-owned energy and resources projects on Australian soil** — Japanese, Korean, European
and Gulf capital is building in the Pilbara, Gladstone, the Hunter and Whyalla. Those clients pay
in AUD, are covered by Australian security-of-payment legislation, require no visa, no local
content certificate and no bond the client cannot post — and, critically, **treat Indigenous
ownership as an asset rather than an irrelevance**, because their land access, Native Title
agreements and Indigenous participation plans depend on it. Offshore, Supply Nation certification
is worth precisely nothing. Onshore, it is the client's single strongest commercial asset. Every
dollar spent chasing Riyadh is a dollar taken away from the only place where their defining
advantage actually prices.

## 1. The Gulf: what would have to be true, and why none of it is

I opened by asking what would have to be true for the Gulf to be a bad market for this client, then
went looking for that evidence first. I found it in every one of the six places I looked.

### 1.1 Saudi Aramco IKTVA — the foreign SME scores approximately zero

| Fact | Value | Grade / source |
|---|---|---|
| Aramco announced iktva **achieved its 70% local content target** | Feb 2026 | [V] — [SPA](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2510083), [Aramco newsroom](https://www.aramco.com/en/news-media/news/2026/aramco-achieves-70-local-content-target-through-flagship-iktva-program) |
| Next target: local content in goods and services procurement | **75% by 2030** | [S] — same |
| Cumulative claimed GDP contribution of iktva | **US$280 billion+** | [S] — same |
| What iktva measures | Value *created in Kingdom*: Saudi labour content, local raw materials, domestic manufacturing, in-Kingdom technology development — **not where the purchase order was placed** | [S] — [vision2030.ai](https://vision2030.ai/encyclopedia/what-is-iktva/) |

Read the last row again, because it is the whole answer. iktva is not a "buy local" preference that
a foreign bidder can lose on price. **It is a score in the buyer's own KPI.** Aramco's procurement
officers are measured on the localisation percentage of their spend. A supplier with no Saudi
payroll, no Saudi purchases and no in-Kingdom facility scores near zero and therefore *drags down
the buyer's own performance metric*. The foreign SME is not merely uncompetitive; it is actively
costly to the person who would have to sign the contract. There is no price at which that reverses.

Aramco has now **hit** its 70% target. That matters directionally: the programme is not a
struggling aspiration that might be waived for a good foreign offer, it is a delivered
institutional success being ratcheted to 75%. The direction of travel is away from you.

|---|---|

| Valid Commercial Registration (CR) number before vendor registration can even be completed | **Fails.** Downstream of the above. |
| **2–3 years of audited financial statements** (or parent company financials) | **Fails.** Barely trading; no audited history exists. |
| ISO certifications for the category (ISO 9001 minimum) | **Fails / unverified.** |

Five simultaneous hard fails. Note especially the audited-financials requirement: it is not
waivable by enthusiasm, a good pitch, or an Indigenous certification. It is a document that does
not exist and cannot be created for two to three years.

completed projects grades at the bottom or not at all.

### 1.4 The RHQ rule — a second, independent gate on government work

Since **1 January 2024**, an international company without a Regional Headquarters licence in Saudi
Arabia is ineligible for most Saudi government contracts **above SAR 1 million (~A$410,000)**
[S — [middleeastbriefing.com](https://www.middleeastbriefing.com/news/saudi-arabia-refines-rhq-procurement-rule/),
[cosmos.global](https://www.cosmos.global/insights/saudi-arabia-regional-headquarters-programme-tax-and-contract-rule)].
By early 2026, **more than 700 multinationals had established RHQs in Riyadh**, ahead of the
original target of ~480 by 2030 [S — [vision2030.ai](https://vision2030.ai/investment/guides/saudi-regional-headquarters-programme/)].
An exemption mechanism now exists allowing government entities to award to non-RHQ foreign
companies "under defined conditions" [S] — but that is a discretionary carve-out designed for
unique capability nobody in Kingdom has. Generic earthmoving is not that.

The incentive attached — **30 years of 0% corporate income tax and 0% withholding tax on eligible
RHQ income** [S] — tells you who this policy is aimed at. It is aimed at Schlumberger and Bechtel,
not at a two-person civil startup.

The UAE gate is structurally identical to Saudi's — **ICV is computed from your UAE spend, so a
foreign entity with no UAE spend scores ~0%** — with one addition: the mandatory attested local
agency agreement. That agent is a permanent counterparty with legal rights over your market
access. UAE commercial agency arrangements are notoriously difficult to exit [U — I could not
verify current UAE Commercial Agencies Law provisions this session; **check before signing
anything**]. An undercapitalised newcomer signing an agency agreement it cannot afford to litigate
is a well-known way to lose a market permanently.

Note also that the ICV certificate is anchored to **audited financial statements** — the same
document the client does not have and cannot have for two to three years.

Note the pattern. **A "gap" that no Australian civil SME fills in the Gulf is not an unexploited
opportunity — it is a market that six sovereign governments have deliberately and successfully
closed by policy.** Every one of these regimes is designed with the explicit purpose of moving
spend from foreign suppliers to domestic ones. Asking whether there is a gap for a foreign SME is
asking whether the policy has failed. Aramco's Feb 2026 announcement says it has not.

### 1.7 The bond: where it stops being difficult and starts being impossible

- Gulf oil and gas tender documents commonly require a **performance bond of 10% of contract value**,
  and bid bonds typically in the 5–10% range (sometimes lower, buyer's discretion) [S —
  [OGDCL master tender set](https://ogdcl.com/sites/default/files/tender%20download/MasterSetofTenderDocument-Proprietary-Foreign-Updated-13-08-2021-BB-PBG-UPDATED.pdf),
  [fcainsurance.com](https://fcainsurance.com/bidbond-tender-10/)].
- To issue any bond, a surety underwrites the contractor's **operations and financial position**
  [S — [swiftbonds.com](https://swiftbonds.com/bid-bond/)].

**[ESTIMATE] Worked example.** A modest US$1,000,000 (~A$1.54m) Gulf subcontract requires a
US$100,000 (~A$154,000) performance guarantee. For a company with under A$50,000 of capital and no
audited accounts, no surety writes that unsecured; the realistic terms are 100% cash cover plus
fees. Required: ~A$154,000 cash locked up. Available: under A$50,000. **Shortfall: ~A$105,000, on
the smallest contract worth mobilising for.** Add the SAR 500,000 (~A$205,000) licence capital and
the entity setup (~A$18,500–61,600) and you are ~A$380,000–420,000 short before the first day of
work.

This is why I am confident giving a hard no rather than a "not yet". Most market-entry problems are
questions of degree. This one is arithmetic.

## 2. Payment risk — quantified, because it is the thing that actually kills them

An undercapitalised contractor does not die of losing tenders. It dies of winning one and not
being paid. So this section matters more than the tender rules.

**Evidence against (stated for honesty):** the Saudi Ministry of Finance **disputes** that payments
are delayed, saying invoices due *"are paid within the contractual time frame"*, that year-to-date
**more than 85% were paid a couple of weeks before the due date and 99.5% within the contractual
timeframe**, and Saudi Arabia reports paying **SAR 525bn** of private sector bills
[S — [Argaam](https://www.argaam.com/en/article/articledetail/id/506828)]. Take that seriously.
But note what it does not address: the *length* of the contractual timeframe itself, retentions,
and the disputed final account — which is where contractors actually die.

**[ESTIMATE] The existential arithmetic.** Suppose the client somehow won an A$500,000 Gulf
subcontract at 90-day nominal terms, paid at 180 days (a conservative reading of regional norms).
Carrying labour, plant mobilisation, freight and site costs would require on the order of
**A$200,000–250,000 of working capital** before the first payment lands (inputs: ~50% of contract
value expended before month 6; attack this if you disagree). They have under A$50,000. **They become
insolvent in roughly month two.** And per the peer-reviewed Saudi figure, the probability that a
given project generates a payment claim or dispute is **greater than one in three**. So the honest
framing is: *a first Gulf contract carries something like a 33% chance of a company-ending event,
conditional on winning it at all.* You do not take a one-in-three chance of ruin for a market you
have no structural advantage in.

## 6. Energy: the real opportunity is onshore, and it is not an export

The assignment asked about energy-transition supply chains, Japan/Korea/Singapore hydrogen
interest, Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Here is the honest disaggregation.

### 6.1 The reframe that is worth more than the rest of this memo

Japanese, Korean, Singaporean, European and Gulf capital flowing into Australian energy
transition **builds assets in Australia**. Hydrogen electrolysers, ammonia plants, transmission
lines, wind and solar farms, critical-minerals processing, LNG maintenance — the civil scope sits
in the Pilbara, Gladstone, the Hunter, Whyalla and western Victoria. **Selling to a Japanese-owned
project in Queensland is a domestic sale to a foreign-owned client, not an export.** It carries:

- No visa, no local-content certificate, no agency agreement, no foreign entity, no MISA capital.
- AUD payment, Australian contract law, and **Australian security-of-payment legislation** — the
  single most valuable protection an undercapitalised subcontractor has, and the exact protection
  that vanishes the moment they invoice a Saudi entity.
- **Indigenous ownership as a priced advantage**, because these projects run Native Title
  agreements, land access negotiations and Indigenous participation targets. Offshore, Supply
  Nation certification is worth nothing; onshore, on a project crossing determined land, it is the
  client's strongest card.

If the client's underlying motivation is "I want to work on big energy projects with international
money", that ambition is fully satisfiable without a passport. Say this to them plainly.

### 6.2 Hydrogen export — treat the 2022-2023 narrative as stale

**[U — knowledge to May 2026, NOT verified this session, do not repeat without checking]:** the
Australian green hydrogen export pipeline contracted sharply through 2024–2025, with multiple
flagship projects withdrawn or shelved and developers cutting hydrogen targets. I could not verify
the current 2026 position because the search budget was exhausted. **Do not build any plan on
hydrogen without a fresh check.**

But note that the conclusion is robust either way: **whether hydrogen projects proceed or not, the
civil work is in Australia.** The client's exposure to hydrogen is as a domestic subcontractor to a
domestic project, and that is unaffected by anything in this memo.

The one Gulf hydrogen data point that is verified: NEOM's **Oxagon hydrogen plant is tracking to
operational status in 2027** [S] — i.e. built, contracted, and closed to newcomers.

### 6.3 Southeast Asia, PNG, New Zealand

**[NOT SEARCHED — search budget exhausted before reaching these fronts.]** I will not manufacture
findings. What I can say from structure rather than data:

- **Southeast Asia infrastructure**: dominated by Chinese, Japanese and Korean contractors on
  concessional finance, plus large local contractors. An Australian civil SME has no cost, capital
  or IP advantage. Prior probability of a viable entry: very low. Not worth research spend now.
- **PNG**: Australian-linked, but security, logistics and payment risk are severe for a company
  with no balance sheet, and the market is served by established Australian and local contractors.
  **[NOT RESEARCHED]** — do not act on my intuition, but do not spend money there either.
- **New Zealand**: the only genuinely low-friction "international" market — Closer Economic
  Relations, Trans-Tasman Mutual Recognition, no visa barrier, same language, similar standards
  [U]. **But**: NZ has no Indigenous preference an Australian firm can access (§8), and I could not
  verify 2026 NZ civil market conditions [NOT SEARCHED]. NZ is a market to enter *pulled by a named
  client*, never pushed into speculatively.

## 13. Cheapest next test — 30 days, under A$500 total

Three tests. Each is designed to *kill* a hypothesis, not to validate it. Run all three; they are
cheap and parallel.

**Test 1 — Kill the Gulf in writing (cost: A$0, time: 2 hours + waiting).**
Email the client's own bank or a surety broker, in writing:
> *"We are an Australian civil contractor with under A$50,000 in capital and no audited accounts.
> What security would you require to issue a 10% performance guarantee — approximately A$154,000 —
> in favour of a Saudi or UAE principal?"*
Simultaneously request written quotes from two Saudi corporate-services firms for a **contracting**
MISA licence including the minimum paid capital.
**STOP CONDITION: if the bank answers "full cash cover" or declines, and the setup quotes confirm
SAR 500,000+ paid capital, the Gulf is closed. File the answers and never revisit them until the
six preconditions in §12 are met.** I expect this test to return exactly that, which is why it is
first — it converts my judgement into the client's own documented evidence, which is far more
persuasive to an optimistic founder than a memo.

**Test 2 — Test the Pacific honestly (cost: A$0–500, time: 3 weeks).**
Contact the subcontracts/BD managers at three named DFAT panel head contractors (start with
**Canstruct**, **Fletcher** and **Clough** [S — panel membership via AusTender PNL2283]). Ask four
questions:
1. On Pacific projects, do you mobilise Australian civil subcontractors, or hire locally?
2. If Australian — what minimum turnover, insurance limits and prior Pacific experience do you require?
3. What roles do you *actually* struggle to fill from local labour markets?
4. What would put a new Indigenous-owned Australian civil business on your radar in 24 months?
**STOP CONDITION: if all three say "we mobilise supervisors only and hire locally", the
crew-export thesis is dead.** What survives is a *people* business — supervisors, trainers,
verifiers — which is a different company and must be decided on deliberately, not drifted into.

**Test 3 — Size the market they already have (cost: A$0, time: 1 day). This is the important one.**
Count, from the last 90 days in their home state: how many civil packages carried an Indigenous
participation requirement, what the smallest package value was, and which head contractors issued
them. Then call the three most frequent issuers.
**STOP CONDITION (inverted): if that count is meaningfully greater than zero — and it will be —
the export question is settled by opportunity cost alone, and this memo's job is done.**

## 14. What to say to the client, in one paragraph

The Gulf is not hard for you; it is closed to you, by design, by six governments, with the gate set
at roughly A$425,000 before your first invoice — nine times your capital — and with a one-in-three
chance that winning a contract is what bankrupts you. Australia's largest contractor lost A$1.23
billion there and left. The Australian Government's own data says 1.5% of First Nations businesses
export, that those who do are much bigger than you, that they go to the United States and New
Zealand, and that they sell tourism, IP, hospitality and product — never dirt, because dirt does
not travel. Your Supply Nation certification, which is your single strongest commercial asset, is
worth exactly nothing in Riyadh and nothing in Ottawa and nothing in Auckland. It is worth real
money in the Pilbara, on a Japanese-financed project, on determined land, next year. Go and get
that. Then, in five years, with audited accounts and a bonding line, ask me about the Gulf again —
and I will give you a different answer, because by then a different set of facts will be true.
